# ADR-011: Multilingual Retrieval and Citation Coverage in `alternative_finder`



## Context

`supplier_documents` was, until this work, 100% English regardless of
supplier country — even for suppliers in Mexico, Colombia, China,
Vietnam, and Egypt. This meant the demo's "hybrid search + reranking"
capability was never actually exercised against non-English source
material, and any claim of language-agnostic retrieval was untested.

The goal evolved in two stages. The first stage (the original ADR-011)
asked a narrow, defensible question: does `alternative_finder` retrieve
and correctly audit real evidence regardless of the language it's
written in? The second stage, covered here, asked a broader and more
product-shaped question: **can we make non-English evidence show up in
a real user's results often enough that it isn't a curiosity, without
inventing a false reality in the data?** That second question is the
one most production RAG systems actually have to answer, and it's why
this ADR is written the way it is — as much about the traps as about
the solution.

## Decision

### 1. Scope: 4 languages, chosen by failure mode, not by country count

Spanish, Chinese (simplified), Vietnamese, and Arabic — not to maximize
country coverage, but because each stresses a different failure mode of
the retrieval pipeline:

- **Spanish** — control/baseline. Already worked reasonably well
  (shared Latin script, cognates, proper nouns survive translation).
- **Chinese** — no whitespace word boundaries; stresses tokenization
  (lexical search over-matches on a per-ideogram basis).
- **Vietnamese** — Latin script with spaces, but near-zero lexical
  overlap with English; stresses the "looks easy, isn't" case.
- **Arabic** — the only RTL script in scope; stresses presentation
  (bidi/text-direction), not just retrieval.

### 2. Root-cause diagnosis: why non-English documents were invisible before this work

The search query (`profile_text`, generated by the Plan node) is always
synthesized in English, regardless of the target document's language.
`$rankFusion` combines a vector arm (weight 0.7) and a lexical arm
(weight 0.3, `$search` on `lucene.standard`). With an English query
against a non-English document:

- The lexical arm frequently returns **zero results** for the document
  (no token overlap) — not a low score, an absence.
- RRF math (`k=60`) imposes a **hard ceiling** on any document absent
  from one arm: `score = weight_present / (60 + rank)`. A document
  present in both arms at rank ≤27 beats a document that is #1 in the
  vector arm but missing from the lexical arm. This is a structural
  property of the fusion formula, not a quality problem with the
  embedding model.

Adjusting `$rankFusion` weights was ruled out as a fix — no weight
combination rescues the worst case (Chinese, deficient in the vector arm
itself), and rebalancing sacrifices exact-term search (certificate
numbers, order IDs) for every other document. The fix that worked: a
three-tier reranker (native `$rerank` → external Voyage API fallback →
unranked `$rankFusion` order), which evaluates true semantic relevance
after fusion instead of trusting RRF's arm-presence bias. See ADR-007
for the reranker architecture itself; this ADR only covers why it was
necessary for the multilingual case specifically.

### 3. The authenticity criterion — and where it was deliberately broken later

The original rule: **language reflects who authored the document in
real life, not the supplier's nationality.** A certificate is local-
language only if the issuing body is genuinely local, or an
international certifier's local branch identified by country in the
text (e.g. "Bureau Veritas México", "SGS (China) Co., Ltd.") — a bare
international name with no country qualifier is realistically
English/bilingual for an exporting supplier. `contract` and `email`
were originally kept in English unconditionally, because they are
addressed to FreshMart's English-speaking buyer
(`procurement@freshmart.com`) — that correspondence would be in English
in real life regardless of the supplier's country.

**This rule was later overridden on purpose** (Decision 5 below) once it
became clear it was the binding constraint on how far non-English
coverage could go without inflating the supplier count. That override is
flagged explicitly rather than quietly folded into "the rule" — anyone
reusing this pattern in production should treat authenticity-vs-coverage
as a real trade-off to make consciously, not something to paper over.

### 4. First rollout: 12 of 146 chunks, `certificate`/`sustainability_report` only

Reviewed 30 non-contract chunks across 17 candidate suppliers (9
Spanish-speaking + 8 China/Vietnam/Egypt) against the authenticity
criterion above. `email` and `contract` were excluded (buyer-facing,
same case as each other). `audit_report` was excluded (FreshMart's own
risk notes, not a supplier document). Result: 12 chunks converted (8
Spanish across 5 suppliers, 4 showcase: 1 Chinese, 2 Vietnamese, 1
Arabic) — smaller than initially expected, because most certificates in
this corpus name multinational certifiers without a country qualifier.
Flagged at the time as a content gap in the seed, not a design decision.

A `language` field (BCP-47: `es-MX`, `es-CO`, `es-ES`, `zh-Hans`, `vi`,
`ar`, `en` for the untranslated majority) was added to **all 146**
chunks, not just the translated ones — an absent field on ~92% of
documents would make "missing `language`" an implicit, fragile synonym
for "English."

### 5. Closing the gaps + the conscious override: `contract`/`email` also translated for the 17-supplier pool

Two follow-on goals surfaced once this demo was in active use: (a) a
citation-level target — real `alternative_finder` runs should surface
non-English evidence with meaningfully high probability, not just have
some non-English chunks sitting in the corpus; (b) broader coverage —
most suppliers, not just a curated handful, should have at least one
authentic non-English document available.

Two things were done:

1. **Closed the remaining `certificate`/`sustainability_report` gaps**
   left over from the first rollout — 11 more chunks, including one
   previously undetected gap (`SUP-CDMX-MX`'s certificate had been
   missed in the original review). Each certificate lacking a named
   issuing body had one added, following the same pattern as the first
   rollout.
2. **Extended translation to `contract` and `email`** for the same
   17-supplier pool (34 chunks) — the override described in Decision 3.
   This was the only lever that moves the corpus-wide non-English
   percentage without inflating supplier count: translating existing
   chunks converts, it doesn't add. Adding new suppliers under the
   original rule would have *worsened* the percentage, because each new
   supplier under the unbroken rule contributes 2 English chunks
   (contract + email) for every 1 non-English chunk (certificate) —
   more suppliers means a *lower* non-English share, not higher, as long
   as buyer-facing documents stay English. `audit_report` was left
   untouched throughout (FreshMart's internal notes, never
   supplier-authored, no exception made).

**Mechanics that matter for anyone replaying this pattern:** every
change was a targeted `updateOne`/`bulkWrite` filtered by `chunk_id` —
never a destructive `deleteMany`/collection replace. `_id`, `chunk_id`,
`doc_id`, and `supplier_ref` were preserved on every modified document,
because `chunk_id` is the value other collections (`supplier_alternatives`
seed citations) and this project's own maintenance triggers reference —
not MongoDB's `_id`. A destructive replace would have silently orphaned
those references.

### 6. The local-permit experiment: extending coverage to 12 more suppliers, without breaking authenticity again

Rather than overriding the buyer-facing-document rule a second time,
coverage was extended to 12 suppliers outside the original pool
(Taiwan, Japan, Thailand, Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Berlin, Hamburg, São
Paulo, Curitiba, Warsaw, Milan, Montreal) via **one new document per
supplier that is never addressed to FreshMart** — a local government
permit or registration (factory registration, food-sanitation permit,
environmental/customs permit, Quebec's real bilingual-labelling
requirement for Montreal). This is *more* faithful to the original
authenticity criterion than Decision 5, not less: this is exactly the
kind of document that would never be issued in English in real life,
independent of the buyer's language.

Modeled as `doc_type: "certificate"` (existing vocabulary, no schema or
backend change). Inserted via `insertMany`, with a `doc_id`/`chunk_id`
suffix verified against each supplier's existing codes to avoid
collision, and the supplier's real `supplier_ref`. No existing document
was touched. Deliberately excluded: US (×4), Ireland (×2), India, Kenya,
South Africa, Toronto-CA, Vancouver-CA — English is the genuine
official/business language in these markets, and inventing a
local-language document there would contradict the same criterion this
step otherwise relies on.

### 7. What actually moved the metric that mattered — validated with real (non-curated) runs

This is the most important finding in this document, and the reason
it's written up in this much detail: **corpus-level percentage and
citation-level probability are not the same metric, and optimizing the
first does not reliably move the second.**

Four real `alternative_finder` runs (triggered on Shenzhen, Rotterdam,
VN-088, and Barcelona-ES, each with different risk types) were used as
the validation set. Findings:

- **Decision 5 (contract/email override) did essentially all of the
  real work.** Every non-English citation across all four runs came
  from `SUP-GUADALAJARA-MX`, `SUP-CDMX-MX`, `SUP-MONTERREY-MX`, or other
  pool suppliers whose *full* document set (certificate, contract,
  email, sustainability_report) was translated — because full evidence
  coverage (3 of 3 criteria backed by a real document) is what wins
  rank, and only the fully-translated pool suppliers had that.
- **Decision 6 (the 12 local permits) never got cited, even when its
  suppliers appeared in the candidate shortlist.** `SUP-WARSAW-PL` and
  `SUP-CURITIBA-BR` both appeared in real shortlists, and both cited
  their *pre-existing English* certificate/contract/email — never the
  new local-language permit. The reason: `alternative_finder` evaluates
  three fixed criteria (`compliance_certification`, `operational_status`,
  `sustainability_practices`), and a factory registration or customs
  permit doesn't map as directly onto any of them as an ISO 9001
  certificate or a capacity-confirmation email does. The document was
  real, correctly indexed, and grammatically perfect — and still lost
  every retrieval/citation contest to a better-matching English
  document sitting right next to it.
- Aggregate citation-level result across the four validation runs: 15 of
  36 cited chunks were non-English (41.7%) — close to the original
  target, achieved almost entirely by Decision 5, not Decision 6.

**The general lesson, stated for reuse outside this demo:** a RAG
system's citation behavior is governed by how well a document's content
maps onto the *specific criteria the downstream reasoning step scores
against* — not by whether the document exists, is real, or is correctly
embedded. Increasing corpus diversity without checking that the new
content actually answers the questions the agent asks is effort that
looks productive in a dashboard and does nothing in production. Measure
the metric you actually care about (citation/retrieval rate against real
queries), not its nearest cheap proxy (corpus composition), before
declaring success.

## Consequences

- Corpus-wide non-English share: 12/146 (8.2%, original rollout) →
  58/146 (39.7%, after closing gaps + Decision 5) → 70/158 (44.3%, after
  adding the 12 local permits).
- Supplier-level coverage (≥1 non-English document available to cite):
  12/40 → 29/40 (72.5%).
- **Citation-level result, the metric that was actually the goal**:
  ~41.7% of cited evidence chunks across four real validation runs were
  non-English — driven almost entirely by the 17-supplier pool's full
  translation (Decision 5), with the 12 local-permit documents (Decision
  6) contributing to corpus and supplier-coverage metrics but not to
  citation frequency.
- Multilingual retrieval works today, in production terms, verified
  end-to-end — not just against synthetic test chunks. Real evaluation
  runs retrieved and correctly reasoned over Spanish, Vietnamese, and
  (in earlier testing) Chinese and Arabic evidence without degrading it
  to `unknown`.
- This depends on a reranker being available (native or fallback). If
  both the native stage and the external fallback become unavailable
  simultaneously, non-English documents revert to pre-fix behavior
  (excluded from most top-5 shortlists) — this dependency was true
  before this work and remains true after it.
- The external reranker fallback (Tier 2) sends chunk text to a
  third-party API when active — a documented exception to the "no data
  leaves Atlas" claim made elsewhere in this project's README, scoped
  explicitly as a resilience fallback.

## Known limitations (flagged, not silently accepted)

- The Audit Node's prompt has no explicit language-independence
  instruction. Every real run observed handled non-English evidence
  correctly, but the guardrail against a false `unknown` degradation
  does not exist in code — more non-English content increases the
  surface area where this could eventually go wrong.
- Two pre-existing date-format quirks make specific certificates
  permanently invisible to the `renew_expiring_supplier_documents`
  scheduled trigger, which only renews a certificate if its
  `valid_until` date appears as an exact ISO literal substring inside
  `chunk_text`: `SDOC-GZH112-CERT-01` (date written in Chinese numeral
  format) and `CHUNK-SDOC-VALLE-CERT-01-02` (the chunk references two
  certificates with different expiry dates; the structured field
  matches one, the literal text only contains the other). Neither is
  corrected — localizing a date format is a legitimate translation
  choice, and forcing every future translation to avoid it would
  itself be inauthentic. The operational takeaway for reuse: if a
  downstream automation does literal string matching against a
  structured field, either write dates in a locale-neutral literal
  format everywhere on purpose (the choice made for all *new* content in
  this pass), or make peace with specific documents falling outside that
  automation's reach.
- `SUP-VN-088`'s certificate is permanently and deliberately excluded
  from the renewal trigger, and was never touched by any of the
  translation passes — it exists specifically so the demo always has a
  legitimately expired certificate to show `alternative_finder`'s honest
  non-compliance detection. Anyone extending this dataset further should
  preserve that one exception on purpose.
- The 3 curated example runs in the `supplier_alternatives` seed predate
  this work and still cite the old English `excerpt` text for chunks
  that are now translated. These are static snapshots, not live
  references — they will not self-correct, and were not updated as part
  of this pass. Anyone relying on those specific curated examples as an
  up-to-date multilingual showcase should regenerate them from a live
  run instead.
- Thai and Japanese (introduced via the local-permit documents) share
  Chinese's no-whitespace-tokenization failure mode. Only one chunk per
  affected supplier, and citation frequency for those documents was
  already near zero for unrelated reasons (Decision 7), so this was not
  independently stress-tested the way Chinese was in the original
  rollout.

## Alternatives considered

- **Widening the language scope beyond 4 languages** (German, Dutch,
  Portuguese, Polish, Italian, Japanese, Thai — the languages actually
  spoken by the remaining ~23 suppliers) to push the corpus-wide
  percentage higher. Rejected as the primary lever: the local-permit
  experiment (Decision 6) partially tested this idea in a narrow, safe
  form and confirmed that adding content in more languages, on document
  types that don't map to the evaluation criteria, raises corpus
  statistics without raising citation frequency. A full expansion would
  have cost significantly more effort for the same non-result.
- **Overriding the authenticity rule for `audit_report` as well** (translating
  FreshMart's own internal risk notes to match supplier language).
  Rejected outright, unlike the `contract`/`email` override — these
  documents are never supplier-authored regardless of language, so
  translating them would not be an authenticity trade-off, it would be
  fabrication with no real-world analog at all.
- **Adding many new suppliers instead of extending existing ones.**
  Rejected on the arithmetic in Decision 5: under the original
  authenticity rule, each new supplier's realistic document mix (1
  non-English certificate to 2 English buyer-facing documents) *lowers*
  the corpus-wide non-English share, it doesn't raise it. This only
  stops being true if the buyer-facing-document rule is overridden for
  the new suppliers too, which reduces to the same trade-off already
  made in Decision 5 without the benefit of building on suppliers
  already partially covered.
